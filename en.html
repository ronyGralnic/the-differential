ENGLISH SUMMARY

One problem. Competing solutions. One test.

The Differential is a method made visible. Each case takes a real news story, finds the problem behind it, and lets competing solution ideas fight it out in a step-by-step elimination. Most fall, each for a concrete engineering reason. The survivor gets full documentation: specification, architecture and design. The interactive site is in Hebrew; the documents below are available in English.

CASE 01

Who holds the switch? AI regulation in the US

Eight in ten Americans want AI regulation, and nobody agrees on which. The question that stays open: if a model goes out of control, who holds the switch, and who is allowed to press it? Six solution ideas were eliminated one by one, each for a concrete engineering reason. The winner, Model Kill-Switch Protocol, uses an M-of-N committee signature, an enforcing gateway beside the model and a public append-only log.

CASE 02

A slow-motion crisis: the end of cheap money

Rich-world governments borrowed almost for free for a decade. Now cheap debt is rolling into expensive debt, and the AI giants are borrowing in the same queue. The damage arrives slowly, then all at once, as in the UK in 2022 and at SVB in 2023. Seven solution ideas set out to see the fast part of the crisis before it arrives. The winner, Private Exposure Registry, lets supervised funds and banks split their exposures into three shares so that three regulators can add them up without anyone seeing a single institution's data, and checks every total against cryptographic commitments.

CASE 03

The quiet deletion: 130 million names

A revision of India's voter rolls removed more than 130 million names from the drafts, run centrally with software that showed nobody who was removed and why, and millions had no real chance to appeal in time. Seven solution ideas set out; most fell for concrete reasons. The survivor is a transparency log for the roll: every change recorded with a reason, impossible to alter quietly, cosigned by independent witnesses, and the person whose name changed hears about it the same day.

CASE 04

Promises with an expiry date

A US president enters his last two years after losing both chambers in the midterms, and turns abroad: Iran, Ukraine, Cuba. Meanwhile the national-security staff thins out, and successors arrive who were not in the room when promises were made. The case judges no policy; it asks a question every government faces: who remembers what was promised, once whoever promised it has left the room? Seven solution ideas set out; most fell for concrete reasons. The survivor is Desk Memory: every promise becomes a thread with a source quote and its maker's approval, threads change owner on the day someone leaves, the successor gets a day-one briefing, and everything goes to the National Archives at the end of the term.

CASE 05

The funnel

An AI developer heads for a giant IPO while trillions are poured into data centres, chips and power at the bottom of the funnel. By one calculation the buildout needs about $3 trillion of revenue a year by 2030; about $200 billion comes in today, and the indirect indicators contradict each other. The case recommends no stock and judges no company; it asks how to see, in time, whether the money will arrive. Seven solution ideas set out; most fell for concrete reasons. The survivor is the Payback Meter: it measures every decode step inside the serving engine, splits shared GPU time by the memory each request held, and writes one append-only row per request with cost, revenue and version. Above the rows: margin by use, a payback clock per model version, a customer statement, and a gate that releases only segments to the market. Disclosure: the site is built with Claude, a model made by Anthropic, the company at the centre of the story.

CASE 06

Who answers

Polls used to reach one person in two; today one or two in a hundred answer, and those who answer differ from those who don't. In a year when the non-responders turn out, the error grows. The case judges no party or candidate; it asks a technical question: how do you know what a whole country thinks when almost nobody answers? Seven solution ideas set out; most fell for concrete reasons. The survivor is Second Call: a poll that sees itself while it is still in the field, moves effort to the cells that are missing, calibrates to a small random-address anchor, returns in a second wave a week before election day, and publishes what it did not see.
